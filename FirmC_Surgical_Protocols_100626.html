⚠ Key Principle A tender, irreducible hernia with overlying skin changes, systemic upset, or signs of obstruction is strangulation until proven otherwise — a surgical emergency. Do NOT attempt forced reduction of a suspected strangulated hernia.
1
Definitions & Spectrum
  • Reducible: Contents return spontaneously or with gentle pressure. Not an emergency.
  • Incarcerated: Irreducible but viable contents. At risk. Urgent
  • Obstructed: Incarcerated bowel causing intestinal obstruction. Urgent
  • Strangulated: Vascular compromise → ischaemia → necrosis. Emergency

Strangulation can occur without complete obstruction (Richter's hernia — only part of the bowel circumference is trapped; the patient may still pass stool/flatus yet have ischaemic bowel).

2
Initial Assessment

History

  • Duration of irreducibility — time is bowel
  • Pain: onset, severity, change in character (constant severe pain suggests ischaemia)
  • Obstructive features: colicky pain, vomiting, distension, absolute constipation
  • Prior episodes, previous repair, comorbidities, anticoagulation

Examination

  • Lump: site, tenderness, reducibility, cough impulse (lost in incarceration)
  • Overlying skin: erythema, oedema, shininess → think strangulation
  • Abdomen: distension, peritonism, bowel sounds
  • Systemic: tachycardia, fever, hypotension, dehydration

Bloods

  • FBC, U&E, lactate (raised lactate = concern for ischaemia); CRP if available — not routinely available in our setting
  • VBG, amylase, coagulation, group & save
3
Red Flags — Strangulation

🚨 Do NOT attempt reduction — resuscitate & theatre

  • Severe, constant, disproportionate pain over the lump
  • Tense, exquisitely tender, irreducible lump
  • Overlying skin erythema / oedema / discolouration
  • Systemic toxicity: fever, tachycardia, hypotension
  • Raised lactate / metabolic acidosis / rising WCC
  • Peritonism

Forced reduction of strangulated bowel risks "reduction en masse" — returning dead bowel into the abdomen, masking ongoing ischaemia and perforation.

4
Attempting Reduction

⚑ This firm operates a HIGH THRESHOLD for reduction

Reduction is not a forceful manoeuvre. It should be an essentially spontaneous event — the hernia falls back with positioning, analgesia and patience, with at most the gentlest guiding pressure. If you have to struggle, STOP. The patient goes to surgery.

The Timeline Rule

< 24 hours The key window

Reduction (taxis) may be considered only when onset of the painful irreducible lump is within 24 hours and there are no signs of strangulation. A symptom duration of ≥ 24 hours is a recognised predictor of non-viable, strangulated bowel [1,2] — beyond this window the threshold to operate should be even lower.

All of these must be true to attempt

Only if ALL met

  • No red flags for strangulation (Section 3)
  • Onset < 24 hours
  • No systemic upset; normal lactate, no acidosis
  • No peritonism or obstructive compromise needing urgent surgery
  • You expect this to be an easily reducible hernia

Technique — "Gentle, Prepared, Safe" (GPS) Taxis

Prepare & give analgesiaQuiet setting, IV access, monitoring. Adequate analgesia ± carefully titrated conscious sedation per senior & local sedation policy.
Position & elevate the limbsSupine, Trendelenburg (head down ~20°), hips flexed — let gravity and relaxation do the work.
Wait for it to come down on its ownGive it time after analgesia. Many hernias reduce spontaneously without any pushing — this is the ideal outcome.
At most: the gentlest guiding pressureIf needed, only light, constant, steady pressure for a few minutes. No forceful pushing. No repeated attempts.
If it does not slide back easily → STOPAny struggle, resistance, increasing pain, or more than one gentle attempt = abandon. Escalate to senior, prepare for theatre.
If it reduces easilyAdmit & observe (≥24h). Watch for reduction en masse / peritonism. Plan urgent repair — same admission or planned urgent list.

Why the caution: Forceful or struggled reduction risks reduction en masse — returning ischaemic bowel into the abdomen, masking strangulation and perforation. A struggled reduction is more dangerous than a timely operation.

Post-reduction observation is mandatory — worsening pain or peritonism after reduction means reduced non-viable bowel until proven otherwise: operate.

5

6
Indications for Theatre

Emergency (within hours)

  • Suspected strangulation
  • Failed reduction of incarcerated/obstructed hernia
  • Peritonitis / perforation

Urgent (same admission)

  • Successfully reduced but recurrent incarceration
  • Irreducible but viable, no immediate compromise — list urgently

Operative Principles

  • Open approach is standard in the emergency/strangulated setting
  • Open the sac, assess viability before reduction — do not lose ischaemic bowel into the abdomen
  • Warm packs + reassess; resect non-viable bowel
  • Avoid mesh in contaminated fields [3,4] (resection/perforation) — primary tissue repair preferred; use surgeon judgement
  • Clean incarceration without resection — mesh repair reasonable
7
Peri-operative Care

Pre-op resuscitation

  • IV fluids (balanced crystalloid), correct electrolytes
  • NG tube if obstructed/vomiting; urinary catheter; strict fluid balance
  • Analgesia, antiemetics, VTE assessment
  • IV antibiotics if strangulation/perforation suspected — per local policy
  • Consent including possible bowel resection & stoma

Post-op

  • Monitor for anastomotic concerns if resection performed
  • Early mobilisation, VTE prophylaxis, analgesia [3]
  • Watch wound for infection — higher risk in contaminated cases
8
Escalation Triggers

Call senior staff immediately

  • Any suspicion of strangulation
  • Failed reduction attempt
  • Rising lactate / systemic toxicity / peritonism
  • Post-reduction deterioration
  • Haemodynamic instability

⚑ Firm C Stance Appendicectomy is the primary treatment for acute appendicitis. Non-operative management is reserved for selected patients who are unfit for surgery, or in whom the risk of surgery clearly outweighs its benefit — and only in uncomplicated disease, after full counselling on recurrence.
★
Firm C Position on Management

Default Surgery first

For the great majority of patients with acute appendicitis, appendicectomy (preferably laparoscopic) is the treatment of choice. It is definitive, removes diagnostic uncertainty, and avoids the recurrence risk inherent to antibiotics alone.

Exception Non-operative management (NOM)

NOM with antibiotics is considered only [1,2] when one or more apply:

  • The patient is not surgically/anaesthetically suited (significant comorbidity, prohibitive operative risk)
  • The risk of surgery clearly outweighs the benefit versus NOM in a selected patient
  • Disease is uncomplicated (no perforation, abscess, phlegmon, or appendicolith on imaging)
  • The patient is fully counselled and accepts the recurrence risk (a meaningful proportion require appendicectomy within 1 year)

This stance is consistent with WSES guidance: NOM is a reasonable alternative for uncomplicated AA in selected patients after informed discussion, but surgery remains the most reliable definitive treatment. The decision is always made with senior staff.

1
Clinical Assessment

History

  • Classic: peri-umbilical pain migrating to right iliac fossa (RIF)
  • Anorexia, nausea, vomiting, low-grade fever
  • Duration & progression of pain; previous similar episodes
  • LMP and pregnancy status in women of childbearing age

Examination

  • RIF tenderness at McBurney's point, guarding, rebound
  • Rovsing's, psoas, obturator signs
  • Signs of peritonitis (generalised guarding, rigidity) → perforation
  • Temperature, HR, signs of sepsis

Bloods & baseline

  • FBC (neutrophil leucocytosis); CRP if available — not routinely available in our setting (see scoring note below)
  • U&E, β-hCG in women of childbearing age, urinalysis (exclude urological cause)
  • Group & save if surgery likely

No single sign or test is definitive — combine clinical findings, inflammatory markers and a scoring system, supported by imaging where needed.

2
Risk Scoring

Local note CRP-based scores not usable here [6]

The AIR and AAS scores both require C-reactive protein (CRP), which is not routinely available in our setting. Firm C therefore uses the Alvarado score as the default, since it relies only on clinical features and the white cell count — both available to us.

Interpretation (guide)

  • 1–4: Low probability — appendicitis unlikely; consider discharge/observe or seek alternative diagnosis
  • 5–6: Equivocal — observe and/or image (CT/US)
  • 7–10: High probability — surgical assessment; imaging selectively

Scores support but never replace clinical judgement. A convincing clinical picture warrants surgical review regardless of score. Because we lack CRP — which adds specificity in the AIR/AAS scores — Firm C leans more on serial clinical assessment and selective imaging to resolve equivocal (Alvarado 5–6) cases rather than on the score alone.

4
Surgical Management (Primary)

Appendicectomy — treatment of choice

  • Laparoscopic appendicectomy preferred where expertise/equipment allow — less pain, fewer wound infections, quicker recovery
  • Open appendicectomy entirely appropriate where laparoscopy unavailable or unsuitable

Timing

  • Appendicectomy for uncomplicated AA can be performed on the next available list — a short in-hospital delay (generally up to ~24 h) with antibiotic cover does not increase complications
  • Do not unduly delay; expedite if clinical deterioration or features of complicated disease

Principles

  • Pre-operative single-dose prophylactic antibiotics
  • Send appendix for histology (incidental malignancy / unexpected pathology)
  • Document intra-operative grade of appendicitis
5
Non-operative Management (Selected)

Only after the Firm C criteria are met (Section ★)

Reserved for the unfit/high-operative-risk patient, in uncomplicated disease, with informed consent regarding recurrence.

🗣 Patient Choice — figures for the consent discussion

If a suitable patient chooses non-operative management, they must be made aware of the recurrence/failure risk using actual figures. From the CODA randomised trial, of patients treated with antibiotics the proportion who went on to need appendicectomy was:

  • ~29% by 90 days (about 3 in 10) [4]
  • ~40% by 1 year (about 4 in 10) [4]
  • ~46% by 2 years, rising to ~49% by 3–4 years [4] (roughly half)

Appendicolith present: markedly higher failure — appendicectomy by 90 days ~41% (vs ~25% without), and higher complication rates (e.g. abscess needing drainage ~7% vs ~1%). An appendicolith is therefore a reason not to offer NOM.

The figures above are for the verbal discussion with the patient — they need not be written out in the notes. Documenting something like "risks and recurrence of non-operative management discussed with patient as per protocol" is sufficient, provided the discussion genuinely took place.

Pre-conditions

  • Imaging-confirmed uncomplicated AA
  • No appendicolith (higher failure & complication risk if present)
  • No perforation, abscess, phlegmon, or diffuse peritonitis
  • Patient counselled with actual recurrence figures (see Patient Choice box above); risk of recurrence and of missed pathology accepted

Regimen & monitoring

  • IV antibiotics per local policy, with step-down to oral on improvement
  • Active inpatient observation — serial examination & inflammatory markers
  • Failure to improve within 24–48 h, or any deterioration → proceed to appendicectomy
  • Clear safety-netting and follow-up on discharge

If at any point the diagnosis becomes complicated or the patient deteriorates, NOM is abandoned in favour of surgery.

6
Complicated Appendicitis

Generalised peritonitis / interloop abscesses / diffuse contamination

This is a surgical emergency. Resuscitate and proceed to urgent open laparotomy + appendicectomy + peritoneal lavage. Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics. Non-operative management has no role here.

Localised right iliac fossa (RIF) abscess — the key distinction

The non-operative pathway applies only to a localised, contained RIF abscess — confirmed on imaging — without generalised peritonitis, interloop collections, or diffuse contamination. Once you have confirmed this, size determines the approach:

FindingGuideline recommendation
Phlegmon (no drainable collection)IV antibiotics alone — no drainage. Monitor closely for resolution over 48–72 h
Localised abscess <4 cmIV antibiotics alone — abscess too small for reliable percutaneous drainage; monitor for resolution
Localised abscess ≥4 cmIV antibiotics + percutaneous image-guided drainage (USS or CT-guided) — standard non-operative approach. Where laparoscopic expertise is available: laparoscopic appendicectomy + drain is a safe and acceptable alternative

⚑ Firm C local position — resource deviation

Laparoscopy is not consistently available in our setting. Firm C manages all localised RIF abscesses — irrespective of size — with IV antibiotics and percutaneous image-guided drainage where a drainable collection is present. This deviates from the guideline option of laparoscopic appendicectomy + drain for abscesses ≥4 cm, for resource reasons.

For abscesses <4 cm or phlegmon where percutaneous drainage is not feasible: IV antibiotics alone, consistent with guidelines.

If NOM fails — no improvement at 48–72 h, or any deterioration — proceed to open appendicectomy as our surgical fallback where laparoscopy is unavailable.

Interval appendicectomy after successful NOM

  • Routine interval appendicectomy is not recommended — recurrence risk is low (~5–26%) and most do not need it
  • Patients aged ≥35: interval appendicectomy recommended at 2–3 months — neoplasm rate in periappendiceal abscess reaches ~10–29% in this age group
  • All patients ≥35 should also have colonic evaluation (CT and colonoscopy) to exclude a caecal or right-colon malignancy
  • Patients <35: safety-net, close follow-up, and MRI at 1 year if not proceeding to interval appendicectomy

All complicated appendicitis decisions are senior staff-led. Call early — do not manage complicated disease without senior input.

7
Peri-operative Care

Pre-op

  • IV fluids, analgesia, antiemetics; nil by mouth
  • Single-dose prophylactic antibiotics on induction
  • Consent including conversion to open and possible findings

Post-op

  • Uncomplicated: no extended antibiotics needed; early mobilisation, diet as tolerated
  • Complicated/perforated: short-course post-operative antibiotics (≈2–3 days if adequate source control), monitor for collection/ileus
  • Ensure histology is reviewed and acted upon
8
Escalation Triggers

Call senior staff

  • Signs of perforation / generalised peritonitis
  • Septic / haemodynamically unstable patient
  • Diagnostic uncertainty, or considering NOM
  • Failure to improve on NOM, or any deterioration
  • Pregnant patient, or extremes of age with comorbidity

⚑ Core Principle Fluid therapy is a continuous loop, not a one-off calculation. Prescribe → give → reassess the patient's response → adjust. Every deficit correction must be followed by checking whether it worked and whether more (or less) is needed.
1
The 5 Rs Framework

Every IV fluid prescription should answer which of the 5 Rs it is for:

  • Resuscitation — restoring circulating volume in the shocked/hypovolaemic patient
  • Routine maintenance — daily water & electrolytes for the patient who can't drink
  • Replacement — ongoing abnormal losses (vomiting, NG, fistula, drains, diarrhoea, third-space)
  • Redistribution — adjusting for oedema, sepsis, organ dysfunction (senior-led)
  • Reassessment — the continuous review that ties it all together

A patient may need more than one R at once (e.g. resuscitation + replacement of NG losses). Prescribe each component deliberately rather than a vague "maintenance".

2
Assess Fluid Status

Signs of hypovolaemia / need for resuscitation

  • Systolic BP <100 mmHg; HR >90; cap refill >2 s or cool peripheries
  • RR >20; passive leg raise suggesting fluid responsiveness
  • Oliguria (<0.5 ml/kg/hour), thirst, reduced skin turgor, dry mucous membranes

Signs of overload

  • Raised JVP, peripheral/sacral oedema, bibasal crackles, gallop rhythm
  • Rising oxygen requirement, breathlessness

Baseline & monitoring tools

  • Weight, fluid balance chart, U&E, urine output (catheter if resuscitating)
  • Reconcile all inputs — oral, IV, drug infusions, "fluid creep"
3
Resuscitation

Fluid bolus

Give a 500 ml bolus of balanced crystalloid (sodium 130–154 mmol/L) over less than 15 minutes. Use ~250 ml and extra caution in the elderly, cardiac or renal-impaired patient.

Give the bolus500 ml crystalloid over <15 min (smaller aliquots if frail).
Reassess immediatelyHR, BP, cap refill, urine output, lungs.
Still hypovolaemic?Repeat boluses, reassessing after each. After ~2000 ml without response → call senior staff (consider other shock, ongoing haemorrhage, need for HDU/ICU).
Identify & treat the causeBleeding, sepsis, dehydration — fluids buy time, source control fixes it.

In suspected haemorrhage, crystalloid is a bridge — escalate early for blood products and surgical control rather than chasing with large crystalloid volumes.

4
Routine Maintenance

Daily maintenance needs (initial prescription)

  • Water: 25–30 ml/kg/day [1]
  • Na⁺ / K⁺ / Cl⁻: ~1 mmol/kg/day each
  • Glucose: 50–100 g/day (limits starvation ketosis — not nutrition)

Example: a 70 kg man ≈ 2 L/day with ~70 mmol each of Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻ and some glucose.

Important rules

  • Use ideal body weight in obesity; rarely give >3 L/day; seek help if BMI >40
  • Prescribe less (20–25 ml/kg/day) in the elderly, frail, cardiac/renal impaired, malnourished/refeeding risk, or post-op (ADH-driven fluid retention)
  • Adding potassium: pre-mixed bags are safest where available. Where they are not (as in our setting), potassium may be added to the bag but only under the safe rules in Section 9 — never as an unmonitored ad hoc addition

Post-operative and septic patients retain sodium and water [1] — over-prescribing routine maintenance is a common cause of oedema and complications. When in doubt, give less and reassess.

5
Replacement of Losses

On top of maintenance, replace ongoing abnormal losses volume-for-volume with a fluid of similar composition:

LossCharacter / replace with
Vomiting / NG aspirateLoses H⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻ → replace with balanced crystalloid + added K⁺; watch for hypokalaemia & metabolic alkalosis
Diarrhoea / high stomaLoses K⁺ & bicarbonate → balanced crystalloid + K⁺ replacement
Small-bowel fistula / ileus third-spaceBalanced crystalloid; can be large volumes — match to measured output
Drain / blood lossCrystalloid or blood depending on volume & haemodynamics

Measure and chart the losses. "Replacement" only works if outputs are actually quantified — estimate generously where measurement isn't possible, then reassess.

6
Reassessment — the most important R

🔁 Correcting a deficit does not end with hanging the bag

Calculating and giving fluid is only half the job. You must come back and assess whether the patient has responded. A prescription without reassessment is incomplete care.

What to check, and how often

  • Urine output hourly in the resuscitated or acutely unwell patient (target >0.5 ml/kg/hour) — the simplest bedside marker of perfusion and response
  • Repeat HR, BP, cap refill after every bolus and at least with each set of obs
  • Daily U&E and fluid balance; daily weight where feasible
  • Review the prescription at least every 24 h — and sooner if unwell. Stop IV fluids once the patient is drinking adequately

⚠ Guard the elderly against overload

Elderly patients and those with cardiac or renal impairment tolerate over-resuscitation poorly. Use smaller boluses, reassess sooner, watch closely for rising oxygen need, crackles, raised JVP and oedema. It is just as harmful to drown a patient as to under-resuscitate them — reassessment is how you stay between the two.

7
Fluid Choice — Balanced vs Saline

Current evidence favours balanced crystalloids (e.g. lactated Ringer's / Hartmann's)

For most resuscitation and replacement, balanced solutions are now preferred over 0.9% saline.

Why

  • Large trials & meta-analyses show balanced crystalloids are associated with lower major adverse kidney events [2,3,4,5] and a small mortality benefit vs saline (most pronounced with larger volumes and in sepsis)
  • High-volume 0.9% saline causes hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis and is linked to acute kidney injury (its chloride ~154 mmol/L is supraphysiological)
  • Surviving Sepsis (2021) recommends balanced crystalloids over saline in sepsis [6]; ESICM (2024) gives a conditional recommendation favouring balanced solutions

When saline (0.9%) is still preferred

  • Traumatic brain injury — balanced/hypotonic solutions associated with worse outcomes [5]; saline preferred
  • Hypochloraemic metabolic alkalosis (e.g. high NG losses, vomiting, gastric outlet obstruction)
  • Hyponatraemia where controlled sodium correction is intended

Practical stance: default to a balanced crystalloid (Hartmann's/lactated Ringer's) for resuscitation and replacement unless one of the saline-preferred situations applies. Use 0.9% saline as the diluent where a drug requires it.

8
Common Pitfalls & Escalation

Call senior staff

  • No response after ~2000 ml resuscitation, or ongoing haemorrhage
  • Oliguria/anuria despite resuscitation, or rising creatinine
  • Signs of fluid overload / pulmonary oedema developing
  • Severe electrolyte derangement (especially K⁺), or uncertainty in cardiac/renal/elderly patients

Frequent errors

  • Prescribing fluids then never reviewing the response
  • Continuing IV fluids when the patient is eating and drinking
  • Ignoring drug-infusion volumes & "fluid creep" in the daily total
  • Over-filling the elderly; under-resuscitating the septic surgical patient
9
Adding Potassium Safely (500 ml bags)

Our setting: no pre-mixed bags, 500 ml bags only

Where pre-mixed potassium bags are unavailable, KCl may be added to the bag — but it must follow strict limits. The two numbers that keep it safe are the concentration ceiling and the infusion rate ceiling.

🔑 The two hard limits (peripheral line)

  • Concentration ≤ 40 mmol/L → in a 500 ml bag, the maximum is 20 mmol KCl
  • Rate ≤ 10 mmol/hour → a 500 ml bag containing 20 mmol must run over at least 2 hours

Exceeding either limit risks phlebitis, pain, and dangerous hyperkalaemia/arrhythmia. Higher concentrations or rates require a central line + cardiac monitoring and senior staff.

Which fluid to add it to

  • Add KCl to 0.9% sodium chloride or balanced crystalloid (Hartmann's / lactated Ringer's)
  • Note Hartmann's already contains ~5 mmol/L potassium — negligible, but be aware when tallying
  • Do not add potassium to glucose-only bags for hypokalaemia correction (glucose drives K⁺ intracellularly and can worsen it acutely)

Mandatory safety checks before and during

  • Confirm urine output — only give to a well-hydrated patient passing urine (≥0.5 ml/kg/hr). Withhold and seek senior advice if oliguric/anuric
  • Check renal function — reduce dose & rate, and increase caution, in renal impairment and the elderly
  • Check magnesium — hypomagnesaemia is the commonest cause of refractory hypokalaemia; correct Mg²⁺ or the K⁺ won't correct
  • Mix thoroughly — invert the bag at least 5 times after adding KCl so it doesn't pool (an unmixed bolus of K⁺ can be fatal)
  • Label the bag clearly with the KCl dose added, and prescribe exact dose, volume, concentration, rate and route
  • Recheck serum K⁺ 2–4 hours after correction; watch for ECG changes; stop if K⁺ > 5.0–5.5
  • Use the largest available vein; concentrated K⁺ is irritant — slow or resite if pain/phlebitis

Escalate to senior staff / consider central line + monitoring

  • Severe hypokalaemia (K⁺ < 2.5) or any arrhythmia / ECG changes / muscle paralysis
  • Need for rates > 10 mmol/hour or concentrations > 40 mmol/L
  • Renal impairment, oliguria, or the unstable patient

1
Assessment — All Obstruction

History

  • Absolute constipation (no flatus, no stool) — key feature of complete obstruction
  • Colicky abdominal pain (mechanical) vs constant pain (strangulation/ischaemia)
  • Vomiting: early and bilious (proximal SBO); late and faeculent (distal/LBO)
  • Distension; prior surgery (adhesions), hernia, malignancy history

Examination

  • Distension, high-pitched or absent bowel sounds, visible peristalsis
  • Peritonism — guarding, rebound, rigidity → strangulation/perforation until proven otherwise
  • Check all hernia orifices — an irreducible tender hernia is your obstruction until proven otherwise
  • Rectal examination — empty rectum (mechanical), loaded rectum (functional/low LBO)
  • Systemic: HR, BP, temperature, hydration — hypovolaemia is common and may be severe

Investigations

  • FBC, U&E, lactate, group & save; CRP if available — not routinely available in our setting
  • Raised lactate / metabolic acidosis → ischaemia until proven otherwise
  • AXR: dilated loops, air-fluid levels, site of transition, absence of rectal gas
  • CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast: gold standard — identifies cause, level, strangulation, closed loop, LBO aetiology. Before ordering: confirm adequate hydration, acceptable renal function (U&E), and no known contrast allergy — withhold IV contrast and discuss with senior staff if any of these conditions are not met

CT is the single most useful investigation. Do not delay it in the patient who needs it. A convincing clinical picture with peritonitis does not wait for CT — resuscitate and operate.

2
Mechanical Small Bowel Obstruction

Operate immediately

  • Peritonitis / perforation / clinical strangulation
  • Closed-loop obstruction on CT
  • Raised lactate / metabolic acidosis
  • Irreducible, tender hernia as the cause
  • Volvulus with ischaemia

Operate urgently (within 24–72 h if no improvement)

  • Complete obstruction not responding to conservative management
  • Virgin abdomen (no prior surgery) — higher index of suspicion for non-adhesive cause
  • Obstruction at hernia site — consider early repair

Conservative management (partial/adhesive — see Section 3)

  • Nil by mouth, IV fluids, NG tube on free drainage
  • Strict fluid balance and monitoring
  • Serial abdominal examination — any deterioration → escalate
  • Review at 24–48 h with imaging if not improving

Bologna Guidelines (2017, updated 2018): initial non-operative management for up to 72 h [1] is appropriate for uncomplicated adhesive SBO. Beyond 72 h without resolution, surgery is indicated.

3
Adhesive SBO — Contrast Challenge

Adhesions are the commonest cause of SBO (~70%). Most will resolve with conservative management. The decision between operative and non-operative management rests first and foremost on CT, which is the gold standard and must be done first. The water-soluble contrast (WSC) challenge has a secondary, adjunctive role.

CT first — always

CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast is the primary investigation and answers the questions that matter most:

  • Is this truly adhesive SBO, or another cause?
  • Is this partial or complete/high-grade obstruction?
  • Is there strangulation, closed loop, ischaemia, or perforation?
  • What is the transition point and level?

These are questions a contrast challenge cannot answer. CT should not be withheld or delayed in favour of a contrast challenge.

Where does the contrast challenge fit?

The WSC challenge is a secondary adjunct, used only after CT has:

  • Confirmed adhesive SBO as the cause
  • Excluded strangulation, closed loop, ischaemia, and perforation
  • Left genuine uncertainty about whether the obstruction is partial (likely to resolve) or complete (likely to need surgery)

In that narrow scenario, watching contrast transit over 8–24 hours on plain AXR adds dynamic functional information — contrast reaching the colon predicts resolution with ~96% sensitivity and specificity. But it tells you nothing CT hasn't already told you about the dangerous complications.

Important caveat for our setting — Ultravist 300

Gastrografin (high-osmolar) has a proposed but unconfirmed therapeutic effect in SBO through osmotic fluid shifts into the bowel lumen. Ultravist 300 (iopromide) is low-osmolar and has no such osmotic effect — its role is purely diagnostic/predictive. Given this, and given that CT already answers the key management questions, the Ultravist challenge in our setting should only be used when CT has been done and leaves specific uncertainty about partial vs complete obstruction, and when the result will genuinely change management. If CT already clearly shows partial or complete obstruction, the contrast challenge adds little.

When CT is unavailable: the Ultravist contrast challenge retains a legitimate role as a fallback investigation to help answer the conservative versus surgical management question. In this scenario it moves from secondary adjunct to primary available tool — use it with the prerequisites and protocol below, document that CT was unavailable, and escalate to senior staff. It does not replace CT; it is the best available alternative when CT cannot be obtained.

Prerequisites before challenge (if proceeding after CT)

⚠ Contrast safety — applies to ALL contrast studies (CT and Ultravist challenge)

  • Patient must be adequately hydrated before any contrast is administered — correct dehydration with IV fluids first; do not give contrast to a hypovolaemic or significantly dehydrated patient
  • Renal function must be acceptable — check U&E before contrast; use caution or withhold contrast in significant renal impairment (eGFR <30, or rising creatinine suggesting AKI); discuss with senior staff
  • No known contrast allergy — always ask specifically about prior reactions to iodine-based contrast before administering. A known allergy is a contraindication; discuss with senior staff and radiology before proceeding
  • Both IV contrast (CT) and oral iopromide (Ultravist) are renally cleared — contrast-induced nephropathy is a real risk in the at-risk patient
  • Document hydration status, renal function, and allergy status before administering any contrast study
  • No signs of strangulation, peritonitis, ischaemia — if so, operate, do not challenge
  • No contrast allergy — ask specifically; a known iodine contrast allergy is a contraindication
  • Patient haemodynamically stable and adequately resuscitated/hydrated
  • NG tube in place and draining
  • CT has confirmed adhesive SBO without closed loop or strangulation
  • No contrast allergy; caution in renal impairment (discuss with senior staff)

🧪 Ultravist 300 contrast challenge — Firm C protocol

Agent: Ultravist 300 (iopromide 300 mg/ml) — non-ionic, low-osmolar water-soluble contrast. Available in our setting; used off-label for oral contrast challenge.

Important note: Ultravist 300 is lower osmolarity than Gastrografin. It does not carry Gastrografin's osmotic therapeutic effect — its value here is diagnostic/predictive only.

Preparation: Draw up 50–100 ml of Ultravist 300 and dilute in 300–400 ml of water (total volume ~400–500 ml). Mix thoroughly.

Administration: Give orally or via NG tube. If via NG, clamp the NG tube for 30–60 minutes after administration, then release to free drainage.

Imaging: Plain AXR at 8 hours and 24 hours after administration.

Interpretation:

  • Contrast in colon by 8 h → partial obstruction, very likely to resolve conservatively — continue NOM
  • Contrast in colon by 24 h → partial obstruction — continue NOM, reassess
  • No contrast in colon at 24 h → complete/high-grade obstruction — plan surgery

⚠ Dose note: This dose is extrapolated from practice-based use of similar non-ionic agents. It has been included for completeness and is pending formal confirmation with local radiology. Do not use until confirmed with the radiology department. Dose to be updated after local review.

After a successful challenge (contrast reaches colon)

  • Continue conservative management — NG, IV fluids, nil by mouth
  • Resume oral intake cautiously when bowel sounds return and patient passes flatus
  • Discharge with plan for elective adhesiolysis if recurrent episodes

After a failed challenge (no contrast at 24 h)

  • Proceed to surgery — laparoscopic or open adhesiolysis depending on expertise and clinical state
  • Do not persist with conservative management beyond 72 h total without senior review

Paralytic Ileus (Functional SBO)

Ileus is failure of coordinated bowel motility without mechanical obstruction. It is the commonest cause of functional small bowel obstruction. Post-operative ileus (POI) is expected after abdominal surgery — the key is distinguishing normal POI from prolonged or pathological ileus.

Common causes

  • Post-operative (most common) — expected for 2–3 days after open surgery; <24 h after laparoscopic
  • Intra-abdominal sepsis or abscess
  • Electrolyte disturbance — especially hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hyponatraemia
  • Drugs — opioids, anticholinergics
  • Retroperitoneal haematoma, pancreatitis, uraemia

Management

  • Identify and treat the cause — ileus won't resolve until the underlying driver is corrected
  • Correct electrolytes aggressively — especially K⁺ and Mg²⁺ (see IV fluids protocol)
  • Minimise opioids; consider regular NSAIDs/paracetamol as alternatives
  • NG tube if vomiting or significantly distended
  • IV fluids, strict fluid balance
  • Early mobilisation and early oral feeding where tolerated (evidence supports this in POI)
  • Chewing gum — low-risk, evidence supports modest reduction in POI duration

Prolonged ileus (>5 days post-op) — escalate and investigate

  • CT to exclude mechanical obstruction, anastomotic leak, intra-abdominal collection
  • Recheck electrolytes, septic screen
  • Senior staff review — consider surgical or radiological intervention if collection

Do not assume post-operative abdominal distension is "just ileus" without excluding mechanical obstruction, anastomotic complication, or intra-abdominal sepsis — especially after 5 days.

5
Mechanical Large Bowel Obstruction

Common causes

  • Colorectal cancer — most common (~60%); left colon > right
  • Sigmoid volvulus — second most common; twist at sigmoid mesentery
  • Caecal volvulus — less common; twist at caecum/terminal ileum
  • Diverticular stricture, faecal impaction

Danger sign — competent ileocaecal valve

If the ileocaecal valve is competent (closed), LBO creates a closed loop between the valve and the obstructing point. The caecum dilates rapidly — caecal diameter >9–12 cm on AXR/CT is at risk of perforation. This is a surgical emergency.

Management by cause

Colorectal cancer obstruction

  • Resuscitate; CT to confirm site and exclude perforation
  • If stable and centre has expertise: consider self-expanding metal stent (SEMS) as bridge to elective surgery (allows staging, bowel prep, one-stage resection)
  • If unstable, perforated, or stent unavailable: emergency resection — Hartmann's procedure most common; primary anastomosis only in selected patients after senior decision

Sigmoid volvulus

  • No peritonism: rigid or flexible sigmoidoscopy for decompression — first-line and highly effective (~80–90%) [5]
  • After successful decompression: plan elective sigmoid colectomy (high recurrence without definitive surgery)
  • Peritonitis / failed decompression / ischaemia: emergency laparotomy + resection

🔧 Rigid sigmoidoscopy for sigmoid volvulus — technique

Rigid sigmoidoscopy is the classic bedside tool for sigmoid volvulus decompression. It requires no sedation, no bowel preparation, and can be performed by the surgical team in the ward or emergency area.

Position the patientLeft lateral (Sims') position or knee-chest position. Ensure good lighting. Have suction immediately available — expect a gush of liquid stool and gas on decompression.
Insert the rigid sigmoidoscopeLubricate well. Insert gently with the obturator in place, aiming toward the umbilicus initially then angling posteriorly following the rectal curve. Remove the obturator once inside and attach the light source and insufflator.
Advance to the point of twistYou will see the twisted, spiralling mucosa at the rectosigmoid junction — the classic "spiral" or "whirlpool" appearance. Do not force through if significant resistance is met.
Pass a flatus tube through the sigmoidoscopeOnce at the twist, gently advance a well-lubricated soft rubber flatus tube or rectal tube (size 28–32 Fr) through the point of obstruction into the distended loop. You will feel and hear a rush of gas and liquid stool — successful decompression. The abdomen will visibly deflate.
Secure and leave the tube in situOnce decompression is confirmed, leave the flatus tube in place — tape it securely to the buttock. It prevents immediate re-twist and allows continued drainage. The sigmoidoscope can be withdrawn over the tube.
Confirm viabilityBefore withdrawing, inspect the mucosa at the point of twist. Viable mucosa is pink and glistening. Dark, necrotic, or ulcerated mucosa suggests ischaemia — abandon endoscopic management and take to emergency surgery immediately.
Post-procedureKeep the flatus tube in for 24–48 h. Confirm decompression with AXR. Plan elective sigmoid colectomy before discharge — recurrence is near-certain without definitive surgery.

Stop and operate immediately if: you cannot pass the tube through the twist, you encounter necrotic mucosa, the patient develops peritonism at any point, or the abdomen does not deflate after apparent tube passage.

Caecal volvulus

  • Endoscopic decompression rarely effective — surgical treatment is usually required
  • Right hemicolectomy or caecostomy depending on viability and patient status
6
Ogilvie's Syndrome (Acute Colonic Pseudo-obstruction)

Massive colonic dilation without mechanical obstruction. Predominantly affects hospitalised patients with significant comorbidity (post-op, trauma, sepsis, cardiac, metabolic). Thought to result from autonomic imbalance suppressing colonic motility.

Diagnosis

  • Massive colonic distension on AXR/CT — exclude mechanical obstruction first (CT with contrast or contrast enema)
  • Caecal diameter on imaging: >9–12 cm = high perforation risk

Management — stepwise (guideline sequence)

📋 Guideline position — ASGE 2020

Current guidelines (ASGE, ASCRS) recommend neostigmine as the first escalation after conservative management fails, with colonoscopic decompression reserved for when neostigmine fails or is contraindicated. Emerging evidence [7] (2025 meta-analysis) suggests colonoscopy may be superior — but guidelines have not formally updated to reflect this yet.

⚑ Firm C local deviation — resource constraint

Neostigmine requires continuous cardiac monitoring and atropine immediately available at the bedside. Where these conditions cannot be guaranteed in our setting, colonoscopic decompression is used as the primary escalation after conservative failure, and neostigmine is reserved for when endoscopy is unavailable or fails. This deviates from the guideline sequence for resource reasons — it is not a clinical disagreement with the guideline.

Step 1 — Conservative (48–72 h)Nil by mouth; correct electrolytes (K⁺, Mg²⁺, Na⁺); stop causative drugs (opioids, anticholinergics); NG tube; rectal tube decompression (multiperforated rectal tube where available); mobilise where possible. Effective in 53–96% of cases [4].
Step 2 — Neostigmine (guideline first escalation — use where conditions can be safely met)Prerequisites: continuous cardiac monitoring and atropine 0.6 mg immediately available at bedside — do not use without these. Dose: 2 mg IV slowly over 3–5 minutes. [5] Patient supine on bedpan; monitor ECG and vitals for 30 min. Effective in 64–91% after first dose [5]; second dose may be given if first fails. Contraindications: mechanical obstruction, bradycardia (<60), active bronchospasm, recent MI, peptic ulcer, significant renal failure (relative), pregnancy.

If monitoring conditions unavailable locally → go directly to Step 3.
Step 3 — Colonoscopic decompression (if neostigmine fails, contraindicated, or monitoring unavailable)Success rate 61–100% at first attempt; recurrence up to 50% — place a decompression tube at time of colonoscopy to reduce recurrence. Requires endoscopy-capable team. Advancing to transverse or ascending colon improves success. ASGE and ESGE recommend this when caecal diameter >12 cm persists despite medical management.
Step 4 — Surgery (last resort)Reserved for peritonitis, perforation, ischaemia, or failure of all above. Caecostomy or subtotal colectomy depending on bowel viability and patient condition. High morbidity in this cohort — involve senior staff early.

Spontaneous perforation risk is ~3% with a 50% mortality [4]. Do not observe indefinitely — escalate early if caecal diameter is rising or the patient is not improving.

7
Operative Indications — Summary

Emergency — operate without delay

  • Peritonitis / perforation
  • Strangulation / ischaemia (raised lactate, metabolic acidosis, constant severe pain)
  • Closed-loop obstruction on CT
  • Irreducible strangulated hernia as the cause
  • Caecal volvulus
  • Sigmoid volvulus with ischaemia or failed decompression

Urgent — operate within 24–72 h

  • Complete SBO not resolving with NOM
  • Failed WSC challenge at 24 h (adhesive SBO)
  • Caecal diameter >12 cm not responding to Ogilvie's treatment
  • LBO from cancer — if stent unavailable or patient unstable

Elective / planned after acute episode

  • Successful sigmoid volvulus decompression → elective sigmoid colectomy
  • Resolved adhesive SBO with recurrent episodes → elective adhesiolysis
  • Cancer obstruction stented successfully → staged resection after optimisation
8
Escalation Triggers

Call senior staff immediately

  • Any peritonism / signs of perforation
  • Rising lactate / metabolic acidosis / haemodynamic instability
  • Constant severe pain (suggests strangulation — never colicky)
  • Caecal diameter approaching or exceeding 12 cm
  • Failure to improve after 48–72 h of conservative management
  • Any deterioration at any point — even if previously improving

  • Consent must be obtained by a fully registered physician — the operating surgeon or a colleague familiar with the procedure [5] — who can explain what is involved, the risks, the benefits, and answer the patient's questions in an informed and appropriate way
  • The patient must have capacity [5] — understands, retains, weighs, and communicates the decision
  • Consent must be voluntary — free from pressure or coercion
  • Discussion must be documented in the notes — what was explained, what questions were asked, what the patient understood

What must be discussed

  • The diagnosis and why surgery is recommended
  • What the procedure involves — in plain language
  • Material risks — those that are common, and those that are serious even if rare
  • Benefits and alternatives including non-operative management
  • Specific risks relevant to this patient and procedure — e.g. stoma formation, conversion to open, nerve injury, organ damage
  • What happens if they decline surgery

Junior staff — know your limits

If you are not confident you can fully explain the procedure and its risks, do not obtain consent alone. Call senior staff. Consent obtained inadequately is no consent at all.

  • Patient is deteriorating or haemodynamically unstable

  • Blood products are not available when cross-match was requested
  • Patient has expressed a change of mind or withdrawn consent
  • Allergy has been identified that was not previously known
  • You are uncertain about any item on this checklist

It is always better to delay a case by 30 minutes than to send an unprepared patient to theatre. Senior staff would rather be called than deal with a preventable intraoperative crisis.

⚑ Core Principle Chest tube removal is a ward procedure frequently performed by junior staff. The decision to remove must be made deliberately — confirm all criteria are met, inform a senior before removing, use correct technique, and observe the patient closely afterwards. When in doubt, leave it in and escalate.
★
Firm C Position on Removal & Clamping

Default — remove without prior clamping when all criteria are met

Firm C's default position is to remove the chest tube without a routine clamping trial when the removal criteria are clearly satisfied. This is consistent with the current evidence base and avoids the risks associated with unnecessary clamping.

Exception — supervised clamping trial in genuine clinical uncertainty

A brief supervised clamping trial of 4–6 hours is a reasonable option when there is genuine clinical uncertainty — for example, equivocal air leak assessment, borderline drainage volumes, or a previous failed removal attempt. It is not a routine step.

If a clamping trial is performed: the patient must be closely observed throughout; the nursing team must know to unclamp immediately if the patient develops respiratory distress, increasing dyspnoea, or oxygen desaturation. Do not leave a clamped tube unmonitored.

📋 Evidence basis for this position

The clamping question has been formally studied. The key findings that inform Firm C's position:

  • A 2025 non-inferiority RCT found that removal without clamping was as safe as clamping trials [1], with comparable reinsertion rates — and that clamping trials caused pneumothorax progression in 13.6% of cases [1], prolonging drainage unnecessarily
  • An earlier RCT (n=180) found no significant difference in pneumothorax recurrence between clamped and unclamped groups [2]
  • One retrospective series found clamping was associated with lower 30-day repeat drainage rates — evidence that cuts the other way — but this was not replicated in other studies
  • A spontaneous pneumothorax series found recurrence within 24 hours was actually higher in the clamped group (17.6% vs 7.4%) [3] unclamped)
  • The 2025 RCT used digital drainage systems not available in our setting — in the absence of objective digital data, a clamping trial remains a clinically defensible safety option in uncertainty, but is not mandated

The weight of current evidence does not support routine clamping. Firm C's no-routine-clamping default is evidence-based. The exception for genuine uncertainty is a pragmatic local safety step, not a guideline requirement.

1
Removal Criteria — Pneumothorax

All of the following must be confirmed before removal:

Ready for removal — all must be true

  • No air leak — no bubbling in the underwater seal drain on breathing, coughing, or Valsalva for a sustained period (minimum 12–24 h)
  • Lung fully re-expanded — confirmed on CXR; no residual pneumothorax
  • Patient clinically stable — no respiratory distress, satisfactory oxygen saturation on air or baseline requirement
  • Drain output minimal — any fluid drainage <150–200 ml/24 h (most pneumothorax tubes drain little fluid; significant drainage suggests haemothorax component — assess accordingly)
  • Underlying cause addressed — e.g. if traumatic, no ongoing clinical concern

Do NOT remove if

  • Air leak still present — even intermittent bubbling on coughing
  • Lung not fully re-expanded on CXR
  • Patient on positive pressure ventilation — removing a chest tube in a ventilated patient risks tension pneumothorax; senior decision only
  • Persistent significant drainage
  • Any clinical deterioration or uncertainty
2
Removal Criteria — Haemothorax

All of the following must be confirmed before removal:

Ready for removal — all must be true

  • Drainage <200 ml in the preceding 24 hours — non-bloody or serous in character
  • No significant residual haemothorax on CXR — lung re-expanded, no significant effusion obscuring the costophrenic angle
  • No ongoing haemorrhage — drain output not increasing, haemodynamically stable
  • Patient clinically stable — no respiratory distress, satisfactory oxygen saturation
  • No air leak — if haemopneumothorax, air leak criteria (Section 1) must also be met

Do NOT remove if

  • Output >200 ml/24 h — particularly if still bloody
  • Residual haemothorax visible on CXR — risk of retained haemothorax and empyema
  • Any sign of ongoing haemorrhage or haemodynamic instability
  • Tube appears blocked — document, flush if local policy allows, and discuss with senior; do not remove a blocked tube without senior review

If a significant residual haemothorax persists despite a functioning drain — CT chest and senior review. Retained haemothorax may require VATS or additional drainage to prevent fibrothorax and empyema.

3
Clamping — When and How

See Section ★ for the full evidence basis and Firm C's position. This section covers the practical aspects if a clamping trial is decided upon.

When a clamping trial may be considered

  • Equivocal air leak — intermittent bubbling that is difficult to characterise
  • Borderline drainage volumes — output just above the 200 ml threshold
  • Previous failed removal attempt with recurrent pneumothorax
  • Senior staff requests trial before removal decision

Removal Technique

Preparation

  • Explain procedure to patient — they need to cooperate with the breathing manoeuvre; take time to explain clearly
  • Ensure analgesia is adequate before starting — removal can be uncomfortable
  • Gather: sterile gloves, dressing pack, sterile gauze swabs, occlusive dressing, suture removal equipment
  • Position patient — sitting upright or semi-recumbent; expose the insertion site
  • Have a second person (nurse) available to assist

Procedure

Remove dressing and expose insertion siteClean technique. Identify the securing suture and the purse-string suture. Confirm the purse-string is intact and the ends are accessible — you will need to tie it immediately after tube removal.
Teach and practise the breathing manoeuvre firstBefore doing anything else, instruct the patient on the Valsalva manoeuvre and have them practise it two or three times:

Option A (end-inspiration hold): Take a deep breath in, hold it, and tense your abdomen.
Option B (end-expiration hold): Breathe out fully, hold it, and tense your abdomen.

Use whichever the patient performs more consistently. Current evidence shows no significant clinical difference between the two [4]. The practice runs ensure the patient knows exactly what to do and reduces the risk of breaking the manoeuvre during actual removal.
Cut the securing sutureHold the purse-string suture ends ready — assistant holds one end, operator holds the other, ready to tie immediately.
Instruct the patient to perform the ValsalvaGive a clear countdown — "Take a deep breath in, hold, tense your tummy — now." Confirm they are holding before proceeding.
Remove the tube swiftly and smoothlyIn one firm, continuous movement while the patient holds the Valsalva. Do not hesitate or slow midway.
Tie the purse-string immediatelyAs soon as the tube clears the chest wall, tie the purse-string suture firmly to seal the wound. Apply sterile gauze and an occlusive dressing over the site. Maintain gentle pressure for 1–2 minutes.
Allow patient to breathe normally and observeCheck the patient is comfortable, oxygen saturation is stable, and there is no surgical emphysema, air leak through the wound, or immediate respiratory compromise.

Post-removal imaging

  • Obtain a CXR or bedside ultrasound within 1–4 hours of removal in all patients
  • Compare with pre-removal film/scan — confirm lung remains expanded, no new pneumothorax
  • Document result and ensure it is reviewed by senior staff

Role of bedside ultrasound (POCUS): Where available, POCUS is a valid and increasingly preferred alternative to CXR for post-removal assessment. A 2025 meta-analysis (15 studies, 3,171 patients) found lung ultrasound had higher sensitivity for pneumothorax detection (79%) [7] than CXR (48%), with specificity of 97–100%. It is radiation-free, immediately available at the bedside, and particularly useful when CXR is delayed or unavailable. The key sign to look for is restoration of lung sliding — its presence effectively rules out pneumothorax at that point. Lung ultrasound for pneumothorax was identified as the number one POCUS skill for all clinicians in the 2024 POCUS25 consensus study. In our setting, where CXR is the standard, POCUS should be used as an adjunct or when CXR is not immediately available — not as a reason to omit imaging entirely.

5
After Removal — Monitoring
  • Observations (HR, BP, RR, SpO₂) at 30 min, 1 h, and 2 h post-removal as a minimum
  • CXR at 1–4 hours — document and escalate if new pneumothorax or haemothorax identified
  • Observe insertion site — small amount of ooze is normal; significant bleeding, air leak through wound, or subcutaneous emphysema is not
  • Ask patient to report any worsening breathlessness, chest pain, or new symptoms promptly

Recurrent pneumothorax or haemothorax post-removal

  • New or worsening pneumothorax on post-removal CXR → call senior staff; may need reinsertion
  • Haemodynamic instability, severe respiratory distress, or tension pneumothorax signs → emergency needle decompression and immediate senior staff
  • Retained haemothorax on CXR → senior review; may need reinsertion or further drainage
6
Escalation Triggers

Call senior staff

  • Any uncertainty about whether criteria for removal are met
  • Tube appears blocked or not draining — do not remove without senior review
  • Patient on positive pressure ventilation
  • Before initiating a clamping trial
  • Deterioration during a clamping trial — unclamp immediately then call
  • New or worsening pneumothorax or haemothorax on post-removal CXR
  • Any haemodynamic instability or significant respiratory compromise at any point

If you are unsure whether the tube should come out — it stays in. Removing a tube that should stay in is far harder to fix than leaving one in a day longer.

Hypokalaemia (K⁺ <3.5 mmol/L)

Common causes in surgical patients

  • GI losses — vomiting, NG aspiration, diarrhoea, high stoma output, fistula
  • Inadequate replacement in IV fluids
  • Diuretics (furosemide, thiazides)
  • Refeeding syndrome
  • Post-operative urinary losses

  • ECG: flattened T waves, prominent U waves, prolonged QT, arrhythmias

⚠ Check magnesium first

Hypomagnesaemia is the commonest cause of refractory hypokalaemia. If K⁺ fails to correct with replacement, check Mg²⁺ and correct it — potassium will not correct until magnesium is replete.

Hyperkalaemia (K⁺ >5.0 mmol/L)

Common causes in surgical patients

  • Renal impairment / AKI — most common
  • Tissue breakdown — crush injury, rhabdomyolysis, haemolysis, bowel ischaemia
  • Medications — ACE inhibitors, potassium-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs, LMWH
  • Metabolic acidosis — K⁺ shifts extracellularly
  • Excessive K⁺ replacement
  • Pseudohyperkalaemia — haemolysed sample; repeat before treating if patient is asymptomatic

🚨 Do ECG first — always

ECG changes dictate urgency. Peaked T waves → widened QRS → sine wave pattern → VF/asystole. Any ECG change beyond peaked T waves = emergency — call senior staff immediately.

ECG changes by severity

  • Peaked (tall, narrow, tented) T waves — earliest sign; often K⁺ 5.5–6.5
  • Prolonged PR, widened QRS — moderate/severe; K⁺ usually >6.5
  • Sine wave pattern, loss of P waves — life-threatening; imminent arrest

Management — stepwise (the four actions)

Step 1 — Protect the heart (if ECG changes present)Calcium gluconate 10 ml of 10% IV over 5–10 minutes. Stabilises the cardiac membrane. Onset within 1–3 min; lasts 30–60 min. Does NOT lower potassium — buys time for definitive treatment. Can repeat after 5 minutes if ECG unchanged. Use calcium chloride 10 ml of 10% if central access available — more potent.
Step 2 — Shift K⁺ into cells (insulin-dextrose cocktail)10 units soluble insulin mixed with 50 ml of 50% dextrose, given IV over 15–30 minutes. [1,2] Onset 15–30 min; peaks 30–60 min; lasts 2–6 hours. Lowers K⁺ by ~0.6–1.0 mmol/L [1,3].

⚠ Hypoglycaemia risk is significant — prevalence 7–75% in studies, may occur 60–120 minutes after administration [1,2,3] and last up to 6 hours (longer in renal failure). Monitor capillary blood glucose (CBG — fingerprick bedside glucometer reading) at 30 min, 1 hour, and 2 hours after administration. If CBG <4 mmol/L treat immediately.

If pre-treatment glucose is low (<7 mmol/L): consider reducing insulin to 5 units (with 25 ml of 50% dextrose) to reduce hypoglycaemia risk — emerging evidence supports 5 units as non-inferior for mild-moderate hyperkalaemia [2].

Note: 50% dextrose is highly osmolar and irritant — administer via a large vein; watch for extravasation and phlebitis.
Step 3 — Nebulised salbutamol (adjunct)10–20 mg nebulised salbutamol (use 10 mg if ischaemic heart disease or significant tachycardia). Works synergistically with insulin-dextrose to lower K⁺ by an additional 0.5–1.0 mmol/L. Onset 15–30 min; lasts ~2 hours. Useful adjunct — not a standalone treatment. Note: may be less effective in patients on beta-blockers.
Step 4 — Remove K⁺ from the bodySteps 1–3 shift or stabilise K⁺ only — they do not remove it. Definitive removal requires: Furosemide IV (if adequate renal function and not anuric — promotes urinary K⁺ excretion); calcium resonium (ion exchange resin — oral or enema, slow acting); or dialysis (for severe refractory hyperkalaemia or oligoanuric renal failure — senior and nephrology decision).

Treat the cause in parallel

  • Stop all potassium-containing fluids and medications contributing to hyperkalaemia
  • Withhold ACE inhibitors, ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs
  • Treat acidosis if present — correcting pH will drive K⁺ back into cells
  • Recheck K⁺ at 1–2 hours and again at 4–6 hours after treatment
3
Hyponatraemia (Na⁺ <135 mmol/L)

Common causes in surgical patients

  • Post-operative — ADH release causes water retention; over-administration of hypotonic fluids
  • GI losses replaced with hypotonic fluid
  • SIADH — pain, nausea, opioids, CNS pathology, pulmonary disease, drugs
  • Diuretics (especially thiazides)
  • Cardiac, hepatic, or renal failure with fluid overload

Clinical features (by rate of onset, not just level)

  • Mild/chronic: often asymptomatic; nausea, malaise, headache
  • Moderate: confusion, lethargy, falls
  • Severe/acute: seizures, reduced GCS, respiratory arrest — emergency

🚨 Symptomatic (seizures/reduced GCS) hyponatraemia — emergency

Call senior staff immediately. Controlled correction with hypertonic saline (1.8% or 3% NaCl) may be required — this is a senior-led decision requiring close monitoring. Target: raise Na⁺ by 1–2 mmol/L per hour until symptoms resolve, then slow to <8–10 mmol/24 h.

Management — asymptomatic/mild-moderate

⚠ The golden rule — correct slowly

Rapid correction of chronic hyponatraemia risks osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS) [5,7] — an irreversible and often fatal neurological complication. Do not raise Na⁺ by more than 8–10 mmol/L in any 24-hour period [5,7].

  • Assess volume status first — hypovolaemic, euvolaemic, or hypervolaemic hyponatraemia each have different treatments
  • Hypovolaemic (low sodium + low volume): cautious IV 0.9% saline — replaces volume and sodium
  • Euvolaemic (SIADH): fluid restriction 1–1.5 L/day; treat the cause; avoid hypotonic fluids
  • Hypervolaemic (oedematous states): fluid restriction + treat underlying cause (diuresis in heart failure, etc.)
  • Monitor Na⁺ every 4–6 hours when actively correcting
  • Correct any concurrent hypokalaemia — K⁺ replacement also raises Na⁺
4
Hypernatraemia (Na⁺ >145 mmol/L)

Common causes in surgical patients

  • Inadequate free water intake — NBM, impaired thirst, reduced consciousness
  • Excessive water losses — fever, sweating, diarrhoea, polyuria (diabetes insipidus, osmotic diuresis)
  • Excessive hypertonic sodium administration

Clinical features

  • Thirst, dry mucous membranes, reduced skin turgor
  • Confusion, lethargy, irritability
  • Severe: seizures, coma, intracranial haemorrhage (from brain shrinkage)

Management

⚠ Correct slowly — same principle as hyponatraemia

Rapid correction of hypernatraemia risks cerebral oedema. Do not lower Na⁺ by more than 8–10 mmol/L in any 24-hour period [5,7] in chronic hypernatraemia (>48 hours). Acute hypernatraemia (<48 hours) may be corrected more quickly — senior decision.

  • Assess and correct volume status first — if hypovolaemic, start with 0.9% saline to restore circulation, then switch to hypotonic fluid once stable
  • Free water replacement: oral water (preferred if tolerated) or IV 5% dextrose or 0.45% saline
  • Calculate free water deficit: Free water deficit (L) = 0.6 × weight (kg) [6] × [(Na⁺ ÷ 140) − 1]
  • Replace deficit over 48–72 hours — half in first 24 hours, remainder over next 24–48 hours
  • Monitor Na⁺ every 4–6 hours when actively correcting
  • Identify and treat the underlying cause — particularly diabetes insipidus if suspected

In the post-operative surgical patient, hypernatraemia most commonly reflects inadequate free water provision while NBM. The fix is usually simple: ensure adequate maintenance fluids include free water, and encourage oral intake as soon as appropriate.

5
Escalation Triggers

Call senior staff immediately

  • K⁺ ≥6.5 mmol/L or any ECG changes beyond peaked T waves
  • K⁺ <2.5 mmol/L or symptomatic hypokalaemia (arrhythmia, weakness, paralysis)
  • Na⁺ <125 mmol/L or any symptomatic hyponatraemia (seizures, confusion, reduced GCS)
  • Na⁺ ≥155 mmol/L or any symptomatic hypernatraemia
  • Hypoglycaemia after insulin-dextrose cocktail unresponsive to treatment
  • Electrolyte disturbance not correcting with standard treatment
  • Any uncertainty about rate or route of correction

Assessing Readiness for Diet

Early oral feeding is supported by ERAS (Enhanced Recovery After Surgery) evidence — the goal is to commence oral intake as soon as safely possible [3,4] after surgery. Do not keep patients unnecessarily NBM.

Ready to commence oral fluids — all should be true

  • Patient is alert and conscious level is adequate to protect airway
  • Nausea and vomiting controlled — not actively vomiting
  • No clinical signs of ileus — some bowel sounds present, not significantly distended, passing flatus (for bowel surgery)
  • No ongoing surgical concern that mandates NBM (e.g. pending re-operation, anastomotic leak concern)
  • Airway protective reflexes intact — not heavily sedated

Advancing diet — stepwise

Sips of waterFirst step — assess swallow and tolerance before advancing. If tolerated without vomiting, proceed.
Free oral fluidsWater, tea, juice. Ensure adequate volume before stepping up.
Light dietClear soups, toast, light foods. Advance as tolerated.
Normal dietFull diet when tolerating light diet well. Stop IV fluids once oral intake is adequate.

Special considerations by procedure

  • Upper GI / oesophageal surgery: diet advancement per surgical protocol; swallow assessment may be needed; do not advance without senior instruction
  • Bowel resection with anastomosis: typically await passage of flatus before advancing to solid diet; early oral fluids generally safe per ERAS
  • Hernia repair, appendicectomy, cholecystectomy: early oral feeding usually within hours of surgery if no concerns
  • NG tube in situ: do not advance oral diet until NG is removed or specifically instructed; aspirate NG before advancing
  • Stoma: ensure stoma functioning before advancing to solid diet

If a patient is not tolerating oral intake by Day 3–5 post major abdominal surgery, consider nutrition support — discuss with senior staff and consider dietitian referral. Refer to the Surgical Nutrition module when available.

Wound & Drain Assessment

Wound — check daily

  • Inspect dressing — is it dry, soaked, or leaking?
  • Surrounding skin — erythema, warmth, swelling extending beyond wound edges
  • Any discharge — serous (normal early), serosanguinous, purulent (infection)
  • Wound edges — apposed, dehiscing, or gaping
  • Sutures/staples — intact, intact but under tension, or breaking

Escalate immediately

  • Purulent discharge, spreading erythema, or wound breakdown with fever — wound infection / dehiscence
  • Fascial dehiscence — bowel or omentum visible; cover with moist sterile dressing and call senior staff urgently
  • Crepitus in wound — gas-forming infection; emergency

Drains — document at every assessment

  • Volume in preceding period — document on fluid balance chart
  • Character — serous, sanguinous, bilious, enteric, purulent
  • Drain site — secure, not kinked or blocked, no leakage around site
  • Criteria for removal — senior staff decision; do not remove drains without instruction

A sudden increase in drain output, change in character (e.g. bilious or enteric content), or drain running dry when a collection is expected — all warrant immediate senior review.

5
Fluid Balance & Urine Output
  • Record all inputs (IV fluids, oral intake, drug infusions) and all outputs (urine, drain, NG, stoma, vomit) on a strict fluid balance chart — calculate cumulative balance every 8–12 hours
  • Target urine output ≥0.5 ml/kg/hour — catheterise if accurate monitoring is needed
  • Review IV fluid prescription every 24 hours — stop IV fluids as soon as oral intake is adequate
  • Post-operative patients retain Na⁺ and water — do not over-prescribe; see IV Fluid Management protocol
  • Check daily U&E in the first 48–72 hours after major surgery or if there are concerns

Oliguria post-operatively — think before giving fluid

Not all post-operative oliguria is hypovolaemia. Before giving a fluid bolus, assess: is the patient clinically dry (tachycardic, hypotensive, poor skin turgor)? Or are they oedematous and fluid-overloaded with poor cardiac output? Fluid in the wrong setting makes things worse. If uncertain — call senior staff.

6
Analgesia Assessment
  • Assess pain score at every review — at rest and on movement/coughing
  • Inadequate analgesia impairs breathing, mobilisation, coughing, and recovery
  • Use a multimodal approach — regular paracetamol, NSAIDs (if not contraindicated), opioids for breakthrough; step down as recovery progresses
  • Worsening pain after initial improvement is a red flag — think complication, not just inadequate analgesia

Opioid caution

  • Monitor respiratory rate and conscious level in patients on opioids — RR <10 or reduced GCS with opioid on board — hold opioid and call senior staff; have naloxone available
  • Opioids cause constipation and ileus — prescribe laxatives alongside; reduce dose as soon as possible

Escalation Triggers

Call senior staff

  • Haemodynamic instability — hypotension, tachycardia not explained by pain or fever alone
  • Falling oxygen saturation or increased oxygen requirement
  • Fever with systemic toxicity — tachycardia, hypotension, confusion
  • Worsening abdominal pain or peritonism after abdominal surgery — suspect anastomotic leak or collection
  • Wound fascial dehiscence or bowel visible
  • Sudden increase or change in character of drain output
  • Oliguria not responding to a fluid challenge
  • Any new confusion or reduced conscious level
  • Failure to progress as expected — not tolerating diet by Day 3–5, not mobilising, persistent ileus

If you are reviewing a post-operative patient and something feels wrong but you cannot identify what — call senior staff. Clinical unease is a valid reason to escalate.

1
Clinical Assessment & Diagnosis

History

  • Sudden onset severe epigastric pain — often described as "knife-like" or "tearing"; rapidly becomes generalised
  • Prior history of peptic ulcer disease, dyspepsia, NSAID or aspirin use, steroid use, H. pylori
  • Nausea, vomiting; patient often lies very still (peritonism)
  • Time of onset — important for prognosis (Boey score)

Examination

  • Generalised peritonism — board-like rigidity, guarding, rebound tenderness
  • Absent or reduced bowel sounds
  • Liver dullness may be absent (free gas under diaphragm)
  • Systemic: tachycardia, fever, hypotension in late/septic presentation

Investigations

  • FBC, U&E, LFTs, amylase (exclude pancreatitis), serum lactate, blood cultures if septic
  • Group & save; coagulation
  • Erect CXR — free gas under diaphragm in ~70% of cases; absence does not exclude perforation

🚨 Pneumoperitoneum + peritonitis = operate. Do not delay for CT.

If the erect CXR demonstrates free gas under the diaphragm and the patient has clinical peritonitis consistent with perforated peptic ulcer — the diagnosis is made. CT is not required before theatre in this scenario. Delaying surgery to obtain a CT scan in a patient with established peritonitis [1] and pneumoperitoneum increases morbidity and mortality. Resuscitate and proceed to theatre.

When CT is appropriate

  • No pneumoperitoneum on CXR — CT is more sensitive and may demonstrate free gas, free fluid, or perforation site not visible on plain film
  • Diagnostic uncertainty — clinical picture is equivocal and alternative diagnoses are being considered
  • Patient is stable and a candidate for non-operative management (NOM) — CT is required to confirm a sealed small perforation before committing to NOM (see Section 3)
  • Planning complex operative approach — anatomy clarification in selected cases, provided no delay to theatre

Remember: contrast safety applies — confirm adequate hydration, acceptable renal function, and no contrast allergy before IV contrast administration.

  • Urinary catheter for fluid balance monitoring

Resuscitation — Pre-operative

Resuscitation and theatre preparation run in parallel — do not delay surgery waiting for perfect parameters in the unstable patient.

  • IV access ×2 large bore; IV fluids — balanced crystalloid resuscitation
  • NG tube insertion — decompress the stomach; reduce ongoing contamination
  • Urinary catheter — strict fluid balance; target urine output ≥0.5 ml/kg/hour
  • IV broad-spectrum antibiotics — start immediately after blood cultures; cover gram-negative and anaerobic organisms per local policy
  • IV proton pump inhibitor (PPI) — given pre-operatively as standard practice to reduce gastric acid secretion and limit further mucosal injury [1]. Note: the evidence that pre-operative IV PPI changes operative outcomes or mortality in perforated peptic ulcer specifically is weak — it is given on a sound physiological rationale and expert consensus, not RCT evidence. Do not delay theatre for PPI administration.
  • Analgesia — IV opioid analgesia; do not withhold adequate pain relief
  • Nil by mouth
  • Consent — explain procedure, risks including re-perforation, leak, stoma, death; Boey score informs risk discussion
  • Inform theatre and anaesthetics — this is an emergency case

Goal of resuscitation: correct haemodynamic instability sufficiently to safely proceed to anaesthesia. In the shocked patient, damage control resuscitation and expedited surgery take priority over achieving normal parameters.

3
Non-operative Management — Selected Cases Only

Non-operative management (NOM) is a senior-led decision, appropriate only in very selected patients. Surgery remains the standard of care for the majority.

NOM may be considered when ALL of the following apply

  • CT confirms small, sealed perforation with no ongoing leak
  • Patient is haemodynamically stable and not peritonitic
  • Symptoms have been present <24 hours
  • No evidence of generalised peritonitis on clinical examination
  • Boey score 0–1 only
  • Senior surgeon has made and documented the decision

NOM regimen

  • Strict NBM; NG on free drainage
  • IV fluids, IV PPI, IV antibiotics
  • Serial abdominal examinations every 4–6 hours
  • Any deterioration → immediate surgery; do not persist with NOM in a deteriorating patient
  • Repeat CT at 24–48 hours if continuing NOM

NOM failure rate is significant. If there is any doubt, operate. A timely operation in a stable patient is far safer than an emergency re-laparotomy in a patient who has deteriorated on NOM.

Operative Management — Duodenal Perforation

Standard approach — suture closure of perforation

Closure of the perforation is the recommended standard operative technique for duodenal perforation. Two main techniques are used — both are acceptable and the choice depends on perforation size, tissue quality, and surgeon preference. There is currently no high-level evidence mandating one over the other. [1,2,3]

Technique — two approaches

Option A — Modified Graham's patch (Cellan-Jones repair) — primary suture closure + omental buttress

What it is: The perforation is first closed with interrupted absorbable sutures, then a tag of viable omentum is laid over the closed repair and sutured in place as a buttress.

Advantage: Primary closure reduces leak risk — when both techniques are feasible, sutured closure is associated with lower leak rates in meta-analysis. More intuitive technique for most surgeons.

Disadvantage: Slightly longer operative time; in friable or ischaemic tissue, sutures may cut through.

Option B — True Graham's patch (plug technique) — omentum without primary suture closure

What it is: A tag of omentum is placed directly over the perforation and held with sutures passing through the full thickness of the duodenal wall on either side — the perforation itself is not primarily closed; the omentum plugs it.

Advantage: Simpler to perform, particularly in the laparoscopic setting or where perforation edges are friable and will not hold sutures; shorter operative time.

Disadvantage: Leak rate 3.3–6.7%; some evidence of higher leak rate than modified Graham's in smaller perforations.

📋 Evidence position

Both techniques are acceptable and widely used. Modified Graham's (Cellan-Jones) is preferred when tissue quality is good and primary closure is feasible — evidence suggests lower leak rates with sutured closure. True Graham's plug is preferred when tissue is friable, ischaemic, or oedematous and sutures will not hold, or where operative time must be minimised. The WSES guideline supports either technique — no formal recommendation mandates one over the other. Use surgeon judgement based on the intraoperative findings.

Approach and additional steps

  • Approach: laparoscopic (preferred where expertise available) or open midline laparotomy — both acceptable; open equally well-supported in resource-limited settings
  • Peritoneal lavage: thorough washout with warm saline — irrigate all quadrants
  • Drain placement: subhepatic drain near the repair — assists detection of post-operative leak

Large perforations (≥2 cm) — expanded operative options

📋 Evidence position on large perforations

Large perforations (≥2 cm) present the greatest operative challenge. The key evidence points to guide decision-making:

  • Omental patch repair for large PPU achieves comparable outcomes to gastrectomy in terms of leak, collection, re-operation, and mortality — yet emergency gastrectomy carries significantly higher morbidity and mortality in its own right
  • Every 10 mm increase in ulcer size increases leak rate 3.3-fold [13] — perforations ≥25 mm carry the highest omental patch leak risk
  • The operative decision should not be based on size alone — intraoperative findings (tissue quality, patient stability, perforation anatomy, surgeon expertise) must all be weighed
  • WSES recommends a tailored approach based on ulcer location — gastric resection for large gastric ulcers, repair ± pyloric exclusion for large duodenal ulcers

  • H. pylori testing post-operatively and eradication therapy is essential to prevent recurrence

Laparoscopic repair is the preferred approach where expertise is available — comparable outcomes to open repair with less wound morbidity. In our setting where laparoscopic expertise may not always be available, open repair via midline laparotomy is equally acceptable and well-supported by evidence.

Operative Management — Gastric Perforation

🔑 Critical difference — always biopsy a gastric ulcer perforation

Gastric ulcers carry a significant risk of underlying malignancy. Every gastric perforation must have the ulcer edges biopsied at the time of surgery — this is non-negotiable. Closing a perforated gastric malignancy without tissue diagnosis delays treatment and worsens prognosis.

Operative approach

  • Small perforation (<2 cm), no malignancy suspicion: excise ulcer edges (to obtain tissue for histology) + primary closure ± omental patch + peritoneal lavage + drain
  • Large perforation (≥2 cm) or suspicious for malignancy: resection with frozen section histology where available; if frozen section not available, close the perforation, take generous biopsies, and plan formal staging and definitive resection once histology is available
  • All gastric perforations: peritoneal washout + subhepatic drain

Post-operative considerations specific to gastric perforation

  • Higher complication rate and mortality than duodenal perforation — monitor closely
  • Ensure biopsy results are followed up — do not allow histology to be lost in the system
  • If malignancy confirmed: refer for formal oncological staging and definitive management
  • H. pylori test and treat post-operatively in all cases
  • Continue IV PPI; step down to oral PPI on diet resumption

Gastric perforations have approximately double the mortality of duodenal perforations. This should be reflected in the pre-operative consent discussion.

Post-operative Care & Post-discharge Plan

Immediate post-operative care (in-hospital)

  • Continue IV antibiotics for 3–5 days post-op (or until clinically improving, afebrile, tolerating oral intake) then step down to oral per local policy
  • Continue IV PPI until oral intake resumes; then high-dose oral PPI (omeprazole 40 mg BD) — this is the better-evidenced component; strongly guideline-supported for mucosal healing and recurrence prevention
  • NG tube — remove when aspirate minimal and bowel sounds return
  • Diet: commence sips of water once NG removed and bowel sounds present; advance slowly
  • Drain — remove when output <50 ml/day and no clinical concern about leak; senior decision
  • Monitor for post-operative leak: rising fever, tachycardia, worsening abdominal pain, increasing drain output → CT and senior review urgently
  • VTE prophylaxis: LMWH + TED stockings; commence when haemostasis confirmed post-op
  • NSAID review: discontinue if possible; if essential, ensure with PPI cover on discharge

Post-discharge — H. pylori eradication

H. pylori eradication is the most important post-discharge intervention

Without eradication, ulcer recurrence reaches 60–80% within one year. With successful eradication, recurrence falls below 5% [8]. This is not optional — prescribe it, explain it to the patient, and confirm eradication at follow-up.

Step 1 — Test for H. pylori

  • Urea breath test (UBT) or stool antigen test (SAT) — once patient is off PPI for ≥2 weeks (PPI suppresses H. pylori and causes false negatives)
  • If intraoperative CLO test or histology was positive — treat without waiting for post-op test
  • If untested due to resource constraints — treat empirically

Step 2 — Eradication regimen

📋 Guideline position — 2024 ACG

Standard clarithromycin triple therapy has been removed as first-line by the 2024 ACG guideline due to rising global clarithromycin and metronidazole resistance [9]. Optimised bismuth quadruple therapy (BQT) is now the preferred first-line regimen.

Bismuth compounds may not be readily available in our setting. Where BQT is not achievable, standard triple therapy remains the practical option — but eradication rates are lower (~72%) and confirmation of eradication is therefore even more important.

Step 3 — Confirm eradication (test of cure)

Mandatory for all patients

  • UBT or monoclonal stool antigen test (SAT) — both acceptable
  • At least 4 weeks after completing antibiotic therapy [9]
  • At least 2 weeks after stopping PPI — essential for test accuracy
  • Do not use serology (blood antibody test) — remains positive after eradication, cannot confirm clearance
  • Positive test of cure = eradication failed → second-line therapy; discuss with senior staff

Why endoscopy after benign intraoperative biopsy in gastric perforation?

Intraoperative biopsies of a perforated gastric ulcer can miss malignancy — the perforation site is often necrotic, inflamed, and yields poor-quality tissue. Published case evidence documents malignancy confirmed on follow-up OGD despite negative intraoperative biopsy [11]. Follow-up endoscopy with repeat biopsies in good-quality tissue under elective conditions is the definitive exclusion of malignancy [11]. A benign intraoperative biopsy does not provide adequate reassurance in gastric perforation.

Discharge summary — minimum plan before leaving hospital

  • High-dose oral PPI (omeprazole 40 mg BD) for 8 weeks prescribed
  • H. pylori eradication course prescribed and explained to patient
  • Test of cure (UBT or SAT) — patient informed it must be done ≥4 weeks post-antibiotics and ≥2 weeks post-PPI
  • Follow-up endoscopy arranged or referenced in discharge letter (all gastric; selected duodenal)
  • NSAID cessation confirmed or PPI cover prescribed if NSAIDs unavoidable
  • Smoking and alcohol counselling — both impair healing and increase recurrence
  • All of the above documented in discharge letter

Call senior staff immediately

  • Any patient with suspected perforated peptic ulcer — this is always a senior-led case
  • Haemodynamic instability or septic shock on presentation
  • Generalised peritonitis — expedite theatre
  • Deterioration on non-operative management — operate without delay

⚠ Vascular Emergency Acute limb ischaemia is limb and life threatening. Irreversible ischaemic changes can occur within hours. Time from onset to revascularisation is the primary determinant of outcome. Call senior staff and vascular surgery immediately — do not delay for imaging if the limb is clearly threatened.

Definitions & Classification
ConditionDefinitionKey features
Acute Limb Ischaemia (ALI)Sudden decrease in limb perfusion causing a potential threat to limb viability — symptoms onset within 14 days [1,2]Sudden onset; no or minimal collateral development; immediate threat to viability; vascular emergency

The 14-day threshold distinguishes ALI from CLTI — onset within 14 days = acute limb ischaemia. The distinction matters because ALI is managed as an emergency revascularisation, while CLTI allows slightly more time for planning. Acute-on-chronic sits between — treat as ALI until proven otherwise.

1

Rutherford Classification — Grading Severity

The Rutherford classification guides urgency and management decisions. Assess sensory loss, motor deficit, and Doppler signals.

Rutherford III — do not attempt revascularisation

A limb with non-blanching mottling, woody rigid muscles, profound sensory and motor loss is irreversibly ischaemic. Revascularisation of dead tissue causes massive reperfusion injury — hyperkalaemia, acidosis, renal failure, cardiac arrest. Amputation is the life-saving intervention. This is a senior surgeon decision made immediately.

📋 Predictive validity of the Rutherford classification

The Rutherford classification is not just descriptive — it has been validated as a genuine outcome predictor. A 2025 prospective study (n=252, 3-year follow-up) found: amputation rates 31.9% vs 7.2% in higher vs lower Rutherford classes (p<0.001); mortality 22.6% vs 7.2% [7] (p<0.001); with the most critical transition point between classes 2 and 3 for reoperations, and classes 4–5 for amputation. In ALI specifically, Rutherford Class IIb is an independent predictor of death or amputation (OR 2.44) [8]. This validates using the classification to drive urgency — the worse the class, the faster you must act.

Most Rutherford validation data uses the chronic limb ischaemia scale (Classes 0–6) rather than the ALI-specific scale (Classes I–III) — the principle is consistent but ALI-specific validation evidence is more limited. The classification is nonetheless the accepted standard and ESVS-recommended tool for ALI management decisions [1].

3
Embolism vs Thrombosis — Critical Distinction

Distinguishing the cause drives the operative approach — embolectomy for embolism, thrombolysis or bypass for thrombosis on atherosclerosis.

FeatureEmbolismThrombosis (acute-on-chronic)
OnsetSudden — minutes to hoursMore gradual — hours to days; often subacute
Background historyNo prior claudication or PAD symptomsHistory of claudication, known peripheral arterial disease
Cardiac sourceOften present — AF, recent MI, valvular disease (85% cardiac origin [3])Usually absent
Contralateral pulsesUsually normal — disease is focalOften diminished — bilateral PAD
Angiographic appearanceSharp cutoff ("Fontaine sign"); minimal collaterals; multiple occlusions [3]Diffuse atherosclerosis; irregular cutoff; well-developed collaterals

In practice, many cases are difficult to distinguish clinically. When in doubt, treat as ALI and proceed to angiography or operative exploration — the intraoperative findings (sharp vs irregular cutoff, back bleeding quality) often clarify the cause.

4
Investigations

Do not delay revascularisation for investigations in Rutherford IIb or III

In a clearly threatened limb, anticoagulate and proceed to theatre. Investigations are done in parallel, not sequentially.

Bedside — immediate

  • Handheld Doppler both limbs — best initial test [1]; confirm absence of signal; compare bilateral
  • ECG — look for atrial fibrillation (embolic source)
  • Ankle-brachial index (ABI) if time allows — <0.4 suggests critical ischaemia

Blood tests

  • FBC, U&E, coagulation profile, group & save
  • Serum lactate — marker of ischaemia severity
  • CK/myoglobin — rhabdomyolysis risk post-revascularisation; baseline renal function
  • Thrombophilia screen — if patient <50 years with no identifiable cause; send before heparin
  • Cardiac enzymes and ECG — if embolic source suspected

Imaging

  • Duplex ultrasound: confirms occlusion level and flow; quick bedside tool
  • CT angiography: gold standard for anatomy; identifies occlusion site, collaterals, atherosclerotic burden; guides operative or endovascular approach. Contrast safety applies — hydration, renal function, allergy check
  • Conventional angiography: in theatre, as part of operative planning or intervention
  • Echocardiography: to identify cardiac embolic source (thrombus, valvular disease) — arrange post-stabilisation
5
Initial Treatment — All ALI
Call senior staff and vascular surgery immediatelyALI is always a senior-led case. Do not manage alone. Notify theatre early.
AnalgesiaIV opioid analgesia — do not withhold. Pain is severe and management of pain is humanitarian and reduces physiological stress.
Anticoagulation — start immediatelyIV unfractionated heparin (UFH): 80–100 units/kg bolus then 18 units/kg/hour infusion, adjusted to maintain aPTT 1.5–2.5× normal [5]. Start as soon as ALI is suspected and before imaging where possible. Prevents propagation of thrombus and protects collaterals. Do not delay for coagulation results unless haemorrhagic risk is high.
Nil by mouthIn anticipation of surgery.
IV access and fluid resuscitationLarge bore IV access; IV fluids — maintain urine output ≥0.5 ml/kg/hour; aggressive hydration protects kidneys from myoglobinuria post-revascularisation.
Urinary catheterStrict fluid balance; watch for myoglobinuria (tea-coloured urine) as early sign of reperfusion injury.
Limb positionPosition the affected limb at heart level or slightly dependent (not elevated) — elevation reduces perfusion pressure; heat lamps are contraindicated as they increase metabolic demand in ischaemic tissue.

Heparin does not lyse clot — it prevents extension and protects collateral flow [1,5] while revascularisation is prepared. It is not a substitute for revascularisation.

Surgical embolectomy — key principles

  • Fogarty balloon catheter through groin arteriotomy [1,3] (common femoral artery) — most common approach for lower limb embolism
  • Pass catheter both proximally and distally to retrieve clot; repeat until good back-bleeding and forward flow confirmed
  • On-table angiogram after embolectomy to confirm adequate revascularisation and identify residual clot
  • Fasciotomy — must be considered after prolonged ischaemia (>4–6 hours) [6] to prevent compartment syndrome on reperfusion
  • Continue anticoagulation post-operatively; identify and treat embolic source (e.g. rate control + anticoagulation for AF)

In our resource-limited setting where endovascular capability may not be available, open surgical embolectomy is the primary revascularisation tool and is well-supported by evidence for embolic ALI. Thrombolysis requires catheter-directed delivery and ICU monitoring — factor in local availability before planning.

7
Acute-on-Chronic Limb Ischaemia

This is the most common presentation of ALI in a general surgical setting — acute thrombosis superimposed on background peripheral arterial disease. Typically older patients with known PAD, diabetes, smoking history, presenting with sudden worsening of a limb that was already compromised.

Key differences from pure ALI

  • Presentation is often less dramatic — pre-existing collaterals provide some residual flow and may partially compensate
  • The limb may not appear as acutely ischaemic as pure embolic ALI — do not be falsely reassured
  • Embolectomy alone is usually insufficient — the underlying atherosclerosis is not removed by a Fogarty catheter; surgical bypass or angioplasty/stenting addresses the disease
  • Thrombolysis is often the first-line where the limb is viable/marginally threatened (Class I–IIa) — it can lyse the acute thrombus and reveal the underlying arterial anatomy
  • Operative mortality and morbidity are higher than pure embolic ALI — these patients have significant comorbidity

Management approach

  • Rutherford classification applies equally — grade the limb and manage by class
  • Anticoagulate immediately with IV heparin
  • CT angiography to map the arterial tree and plan the revascularisation approach
  • Class I–IIa: catheter-directed thrombolysis to lyse acute thrombus; then angioplasty/stenting of underlying stenosis
  • Class IIb: emergency surgical bypass or hybrid approach; embolectomy alone unlikely to be sufficient
  • Class III: amputation
  • Optimise all cardiovascular risk factors post-revascularisation — antiplatelet therapy, statin, blood pressure, diabetes management
8
Complications to Anticipate

Reperfusion injury — anticipate in all revascularised limbs

On restoration of blood flow, ischaemic muscle releases myoglobin, potassium, and metabolic acids. This causes: hyperkalaemia (cardiac arrhythmia risk), metabolic acidosis, acute kidney injury (myoglobin precipitation), systemic inflammatory response. Monitor closely post-revascularisation.

Post-revascularisation monitoring

  • Monitor urine output hourly — target ≥0.5 ml/kg/hr; tea-coloured urine = myoglobinuria → aggressive IV hydration
  • ECG monitoring — hyperkalaemia may cause arrhythmia; treat per electrolyte protocol
  • Repeat U&E, creatinine, CK/myoglobin at 4 and 12 hours post-revascularisation
  • Alkalinise urine if myoglobinuria confirmed — sodium bicarbonate IV (senior decision)
  • Assess limb hourly — improving warmth, colour, return of Doppler signal, pain resolution

Compartment syndrome

A serious and preventable complication after revascularisation of a significantly ischaemic limb. Reperfusion oedema raises intracompartmental pressure → secondary ischaemia. Signs: severe pain on passive stretch, tense swollen compartments, paraesthesia, weakness. Prophylactic or therapeutic 4-compartment fasciotomy is indicated after prolonged ischaemia (>4–6 hours) [6] — senior decision; do not wait for signs to be obvious.

  • Overall mortality of ALI is approximately 20% at 30 days [1,3] — largely from the underlying cardiac disease that caused the embolism
  • Amputation rate remains 10–30% even after revascularisation [1,4] — realistic prognosis discussion with patient and family is essential
9
Escalation Triggers

Call senior staff immediately — all ALI is senior-led

  • Any suspected acute limb ischaemia — do not manage alone
  • Motor deficit or paralysis — Rutherford IIb or III; emergency theatre
  • Non-blanching mottling + woody muscles — Rutherford III; do not revascularise; amputation decision
  • Haemodynamic instability — concurrent cardiac event or massive embolism
  • Post-revascularisation deterioration — worsening ischaemia, loss of Doppler signal, rising compartment pressure
  • Tea-coloured urine / rising creatinine post-revascularisation — myoglobinuria; aggressive management
  • Hyperkalaemia or arrhythmia post-revascularisation — reperfusion injury

Insulin

Fixed-rate IV insulin infusion 0.1 units/kg/hour (soluble insulin in 0.9% NaCl). Start after fluids. No bolus. No sliding scale in DKA.

Potassium

Once K⁺ known & urine output established: K⁺ 3.5–5.5 → add 40 mmol/L; K⁺ <3.5 → senior review urgently; K⁺ >5.5 → no KCl yet. Recheck every 2 h.
